Not a needs-based, but rather [[Motivation#Theories of Motivation|process-based]]. People decide what's best for them, ET seeks to explain how.
People ask themselves 3 Questions when offered an [[Incentives|incentive]]:
1. Will hard work lead to the right outcome? (is this feasible?)
2. Will reaching the goal deliver the reward? (will I gain something from doing this?)
3. Will the reward be valuable to me? (will what i get be something i want/like?)
Each question is given a term:

| Expectancy                    | Instrumentality                          | Valence                 |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| aka effort, 1                 | aka performance, 2                       | aka reward, 3           |
| do i expect to reach the goal | if i reach the goal do i get the reward? | is it worth the effort? |

if ANY question has an answer of no, then the incentive offers ZERO motivation.
1. if i can't reach it, why try? (expectancy)
2. if i don't get the reward after working, why try? (instrumentality)
3. if the reward isn't valuable to me, why try? (valence)

Given a past metric (best week ever), people in a company believe that that is the highest they can possibly accomplish. As the goal increases, the units produced does increase, but only up under a certain limit. After 100 units, they don't have an answer to if its possible (expectancy) and thus give up, making even less units that before. If it never happened before, they don't think it'd happen now.

Thus any goals you make should answer yes to each of the three questions. Make goals that will push employees, but not past the breaking point. Understand that everyone is different, and each has their own expectations on the project. Find the goldilocks zone.

It takes experience to know where to set the goals for employees.

This is process-based, as well as [[Equity Theory]] :0
