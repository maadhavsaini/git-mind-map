## Upside vs Downside
1. Upside Risk: how much better the situation could possibly get
2. Downside Risk: how much worse the situation could possibly get
> If you take more risks, you gain more [[Competitive Advantage]] and have the chance at gaining more upside. You learn, adapt, and get better over time. You need to take risks, just not all.

when there's more certainty, there's less risk, and objective probabilities are there. when there's less certainty, there's more risk, and you have to deal with subjective probabilities. 
## Decision Types
**Programmed**: Low risk, easy, can be made by anyone
**Non-Programmed**: high risk, requires deep thinking, done by seniors

When businesses deal with risk, they may act:
1. Rational: based on data given, they optimize and make the best possible choice[^1]
2. Bounded Rational: use the first solution that works[^2]

To conduct these actions, we use:
**Data Mining**: take huge amounts of data, run it through algorithms to find relationships 
**Regression Analysis**: predict future events, quantitative, needs past history in order to determine future events

Everyone takes different amounts of risks. The more risk-averse you are, the less comfortable you are with taking risks.

## Why do Businesses take Risks?
1. They have ample resources (they have enough resources to survive the downside)
2. They have more info on the risk (the more you know, the less risk there is for you)
3. They have nothing less to lose (you might as well do it, since you're losing anyways)

–
You CANNOT eliminate risk, so managers have to understand how to deal with it. So, take as much risk as you need to, but not what you don't need to take. 

[^1]: used when companies have a lot of time and money, and are doing well
[^2]: used when companies are in a hurry - this is quick, gets to the point as long as it works
